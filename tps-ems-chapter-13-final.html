The TPS Scoring Scale

Every response you deliver in a board room exists on a behavioral performance continuum. The TPS 1/5–5/5 scale defines what each level looks and sounds like — and the score caps are what keep technically correct answers from reaching the top scores when critical elements are missing. Know these before you walk in.

◆ TPS SCORING SCALE — 1/5 THROUGH 5/5
Behavioral Definitions and Score Caps
The score caps are as important as the frameworks themselves. A brilliant analysis that omits the TCHOEDS close cannot reach 5/5. An answer with the right framework but no application cannot reach 4/5. These aren't penalties — they're the definitions of what those scores require. Build your responses to clear them.

The NEMSMA Confidence Spectrum

The NEMSMA framework evaluates EMS leadership responses on a Confidence Spectrum — assessing how clearly and deliberately you demonstrate evidence-based, standard-anchored judgment. This is distinct from personality confidence. What the spectrum measures is whether your answer is grounded in specific standards, stated with appropriate certainty, and delivered without unnecessary hedging. Understanding where your responses land on this spectrum is part of knowing how EMS promotional environments evaluate leadership readiness.

◆ NEMSMA CONFIDENCE SPECTRUM — LEADERSHIP JUDGMENT EVALUATION
Where Your Responses Land — and What That Signals to Evaluators
Three levels. Confident is the target — standard-anchored, clear, appropriately certain. Overconfident overshoots: authority without substance. Underconfident undershoots: hesitation about things you should know at your rank. Preparation closes the gap between where you are and Confident.
Overconfident
The response overstates certainty — asserting positions beyond what the evidence supports, dismissing legitimate complexity, or projecting authority without the substance to back it up. Overconfidence reads as clinical or organizational arrogance to evaluators. It signals poor self-awareness, a pattern that creates real risk at every rank. The officer who is overconfident in the board room is the one who is overconfident on scene and in governance decisions.
Confident ✓ Target
This is where you want to land on every response. Your answer is anchored to specific standards — clinical protocols, NEMSMA pillars, organizational policy, or named frameworks. Judgment is delivered clearly, with appropriate acknowledgment of complexity where it genuinely exists, without hedging things that should not be hedged. You know the standard, you know the answer, and you say so. Evidence and principle are both present. The evaluator has no doubt about your command presence or readiness for the rank being tested.
Underconfident
The response reveals hesitation about things you should know at this rank. Excessive qualification, "it depends" framing without defining what it depends on, inability to state a clear recommendation. Underconfidence signals to evaluators that you may not be able to hold a standard under pressure — which is precisely the condition that leadership scenarios are designed to test. The antidote is not personality — it is preparation. When you know the framework and have practiced the response, confidence follows.
The TPS 1/5–5/5 scale and the NEMSMA Confidence Spectrum reward the same behaviors — because they are measuring the same thing from two angles. A 5/5 TPS response is almost always Highly Confident on the NEMSMA spectrum. Named framework, full application, TCHOEDS close, rank-calibrated delivery: that is both 5/5 and Highly Confident. Prepare for one and you've prepared for both.

The SPARR Practice Methodology

Framework knowledge without practice automaticity produces 3/5 responses. SPARR is the system that builds L·E·A·D — your TPS pre-response execution sequence — to the point where it runs in ten seconds without conscious effort, even when the pressure in the room is high.

◆ SPARR — THE TPS DELIBERATE PRACTICE METHODOLOGY
Building L·E·A·D to Automaticity
S — Spot
Identify Type & Framework
Before answering: identify the question bucket and primary TPS framework. This is the L and E of L·E·A·D running in real time. Takes 10 seconds. Cannot be skipped without sacrificing structure.
P — Picture
Visualize the 5/5 Response
Before speaking: mentally see the complete response — opening framework label, action content (50%+), TCHOEDS close. What does 5/5 sound like for this specific question? Build it in your mind before it leaves your mouth.
A — Actively Practice
Run the Full Rep
Deliver the complete response with full structure — aloud, timed. Not in your head. The voice, the pace, the pause at the TCHOEDS close — all of it must be practiced out loud to build automaticity.
R — Run It Live
Simulate Board Conditions
Timed. Observed (by a peer, mentor, or camera). Standing or seated as you will be in the actual board. The arousal level of observation is what the Yerkes-Dodson curve requires for transfer to actual performance. Practice in comfort does not prepare for performance under pressure.
R — Repeat
One Specific Adjustment
Immediate repetition of the same question with one specific change — the CLEAR growth edge from the previous rep. Not a complete redo — a targeted adjustment. This is how the reps compound: not volume, but deliberate correction.
SPARR Loop
Five Reps per Session
Five full SPARR reps per practice session (different questions each time) builds more preparation value than reading frameworks for an hour. The framework must become automatic. SPARR is the architecture that makes that happen within 2–3 weeks of consistent practice.

🔥 Fire Framework3 U's Diagnostic — Run This Before Any Personnel Action
UNAWARE · UNABLE · UNWILLING
The 3 U's is the single most important diagnostic tool in the TPS personnel library. It determines the correct intervention category before any action is taken. Unaware: does not know the behavior is a problem → communicate clearly, not discipline. Unable: knows but lacks skill/resources → coach, train, support, not discipline. Unwilling: knows, can, chooses not to → the only situation where discipline applies.
Applied to S–1: Two informal mentions have been given. If the behavior continues, the 3 U's diagnosis is Unwilling — the provider knows the standard and is choosing not to meet it. This diagnosis unlocks the formal accountability response. A Supervising Officer who cannot name the diagnosis before naming the intervention is answering at 3/5.
◆ MODELED RESPONSE — 3 U's → L·E·A·D → CLEAR
Diagnosing Before Acting
Diagnose (3 U's)
Before I take any action, I run the 3 U's. Two informal mentions means Unaware and Unable are off the table — this provider knows the expectation. The continued behavior indicates Unwilling, which is the only diagnosis that activates formal accountability. This diagnosis determines everything that follows.
Label (L·E·A·D)
I open the conversation by naming the observable behavior specifically — not interpretation, not character: "Your ePCRs for the last three shifts were submitted more than 24 hours after the call closed, which is outside our policy standard." The SBI model: Situation, Behavior, Impact. I state all three.
Engage & Apply
Using CALM: I Clarify, Ask, and Listen before directing. Even with an Unwilling diagnosis, I confirm I haven't missed something that changes the picture. Then I Apply the CLEAR structure: Commend genuine strengths, state Limitations with specific Examples, define the Adjustment required, and connect to Rank — "as a paramedic, your documentation is a patient safety record, not an administrative courtesy."
Deliver & Document
I document the conversation — date, content, expectation stated. I set a 30-day checkpoint and communicate it explicitly. I name the next step: if compliance does not change, the next conversation involves HR and a written corrective action. Two informal conversations have ended. This conversation opens the formal accountability phase. I say that clearly.
Weak Response to Avoid (3/5 ceiling)
"I'd have another conversation and explain why documentation matters." Fails on two dimensions: (1) no 3 U's diagnosis — the answer jumps to action without identifying why the behavior continues; (2) a third informal conversation signals avoidance of the formal accountability step. The board is specifically testing whether this supervisor escalates appropriately when informal approaches fail.

🔥 Fire FrameworkGlasl Escalation Ladder — Diagnose Before Intervening
PHASE 1: Win-Win · PHASE 2: Win-Lose · PHASE 3: Lose-Lose
Glasl maps nine conflict escalation stages across three phases. The critical principle: the phase determines the intervention. Applying Phase 2 tools to a Phase 1 conflict escalates it. The Supervising Officer's first task is to identify where on the ladder the conflict currently sits — before choosing the intervention. A Phase 1 conflict (hardening, debate, actions) is addressable through direct conversation and conduct standard-setting. A Phase 2 conflict (image formation, coalitions, face-saving) requires more structured intervention. Phase 3 requires HR escalation immediately.
Applied to S–2: Station tension without described policy violations suggests Phase 1 or early Phase 2. The CALM intervention is appropriate. If the conflict has produced harassment allegations, documentation of falsehoods, or crew coalition formation, the diagnosis shifts to Phase 2 and HR must be involved from the outset.
◆ MODELED RESPONSE — GLASL → CALM
Stage the Conflict Before Intervening
Glasl Diagnosis
My first step is to determine the conflict phase. Based on the scenario — station tension, affected working relationship — I am operating in Phase 1 (Win-Win territory). There is no described policy violation, no coalition formation, no documented safety impact. This determines that direct supervisory intervention through CALM is appropriate without immediate HR escalation. I note this assessment can change rapidly.
CALM — Individual First
I meet with each crew member privately before any joint conversation — never together first. Clarify the facts from each perspective without accepting either narrative as complete. Acknowledge the experience genuinely — not to take sides, but to demonstrate that each person has been heard before any expectation is set. CALM governs both conversations: Clarify, Acknowledge, Lead, Monitor.
L·E·A·D Standard
After individual conversations, I Apply the professional conduct standard using L·E·A·D: Label the behavior (tension that affects crew function), Engage with genuine curiosity about what's driving it, Apply the standard equally to both — personal conflict is private; professional conduct on this crew is non-negotiable regardless of personal feelings. Deliver: I set a checkpoint and document both conversations.
Escalation Trigger
If the conflict has produced any conduct that could constitute harassment, hostile work environment, or safety risk, I escalate to HR immediately regardless of the parties' preferences. The Glasl Phase 2 trigger — coalition formation, public face-saving, documented manipulation — is a non-discretionary HR referral at the supervisory level.

"I'd get them together and have them work it out." No Glasl diagnosis. No individual conversations first. No documentation. Boards immediately recognize this as an escalation risk, not a resolution technique.

After the scene is stabilized, the Skill/Will Matrix determines what the debrief conversation looks like. Your paramedic is technically skilled — High Skill — but displaying a situational Will failure under stress — not a knowledge deficit. High Skill / Low Will = Motivate, not Coach. The debrief does not re-teach behavioral health communication techniques — it addresses what happened emotionally and motivationally on this call that produced the behavior. ◆ MODELED RESPONSE — STAR-P
Situation
My technically skilled paramedic is using a tone that is escalating an already agitated behavioral health patient. Patient safety is the immediate priority — secondary is the coaching opportunity. The scene comes before the debrief, always.
Task
As Supervising Officer, I have both the authority and the obligation to intervene. I cannot correct a provider in front of a patient — that destabilizes the scene and destroys the therapeutic relationship. Intervention must be smooth and immediate.
Action
I step in directly: "I've got this one." Lower tone, slower pace, de-escalation posture. After transport and before end of shift, I use the Skill/Will Matrix: High Skill/Low Will — this is a motivational conversation, not a training one. CALM structure: Clarify what happened for them emotionally on the call, Acknowledge the difficulty, Lead to the standard, Monitor with a defined checkpoint. Document.
Result
Patient safety protected immediately. Provider receives coaching in a form that develops rather than humiliates — because I've diagnosed the problem correctly (motivation, not skill) before prescribing the intervention.
Principle
Courage to intervene when a provider needs it. Humility to let them lead when safe. Service — the patient's safety and dignity are the standard every action is measured against, unconditionally.

🔥 Fire FrameworkFour-P Opening — The Structure for Developmental Conversations
PURPOSE · PROBLEM · POSITION · PLAN
The Four-P Opening is the structure for every formal counseling and coaching conversation: Purpose (why we are here), Problem (the specific observable behavior), Position (your role and responsibility as the officer), Plan (what this conversation will accomplish). It is most visible in the counseling context — but it also models how to frame this board response: open with purpose (what changed), identify the problem you faced, state your position (what you believed at the time), describe the plan that emerged from the learning. The parallel is exact.
Applied to S–4: Structure your leadership narrative with the Four-P architecture. This gives the board a clear frame for your story — and models the very supervisory discipline they are assessing you for. Answering a leadership philosophy question using the same structure you'd use in a formal counseling conversation is demonstrating identity, not just knowledge.

This question requires a genuine personal example — the structure is here, but the content has to come from your actual experience. The board is assessing authenticity, self-awareness, and growth orientation. A formulaic answer that doesn't reflect real reflection will be recognized within about thirty seconds. Use something real.

◆ STAR-P STRUCTURE FOR S–4
What Each Component Must Accomplish at the Supervising Level
Situation
Describe a real situation where you were wrong, missed something, or learned something unexpected about your own approach. The moment of genuine learning must be identifiable — "a time I supervised well" is not what this question asks for. It asks for change in you.
Task + Action
Describe what you did — including the thing that turned out to be wrong, incomplete, or needing revision. Intellectual honesty is a 5/5 signal. If you can't describe your own leadership failures, the board will sense you haven't grown from them.
Result + Principle

The TCHOEDS Principle should name Humility or Ownership — and should feel earned by the story, not appended.

TECHNICAL: Known solution · ADAPTIVE: Requires learning + behavior change

🔥 Fire FrameworkWeingarten Rights + Temporal Proximity — Labor Law Awareness

HR consultation is not optional — it is standard practice.

This lesson integrates the Three Ethical Frameworks (Deontological, Consequentialist, Virtue Ethics) and the Newspaper Test to each ethical dilemma scenario. These tools let you articulate why the right answer is right — not just what it is. When a board asks "why did you make that decision?", you need an ethical reasoning framework, not just a values statement. Naming the framework that governs your ethical conclusion is what 5/5 looks like.
◆ THREE ETHICAL FRAMEWORKS — THE REASONING ARCHITECTURE
Every Ethical Dilemma Can Be Analyzed Through Three Lenses
A 5/5 ethical dilemma response doesn't just state the right action — it articulates the ethical reasoning behind it. Name the framework. Apply it explicitly. The board is assessing whether this candidate's ethical conclusions are principled or merely instinctual.
🔥 Deontological (Kantian)
Rules, Duties, Rights
Some actions are inherently wrong regardless of consequences. The duty to report misconduct, to tell the truth, and to follow lawful policy exists independent of whether it produces a good outcome. "What is the rule? What is my duty?" This framework is strongest when defending non-negotiable standards that must hold even when the outcome is uncertain.
◈ Consequentialist (Utilitarian)
Outcomes, Greatest Good
Outcomes matter. The action that produces the greatest benefit for the greatest number of stakeholders is the right one. "What produces the best result?" This framework is most useful when evaluating competing goods — resource allocation decisions, triage ethics, disclosure decisions where multiple parties are affected differently by different choices.
◆ Virtue Ethics
Character, Identity, Integrity
The right action is what a person of good character would do. "What would an officer of integrity do in this situation?" This framework is the closest to TCHOEDS — it asks not what the rules require or what the outcomes produce, but what kind of officer you are revealing yourself to be through this decision. It is the foundation of the Identity Anchor.
The Newspaper Test: "Would I be comfortable if this decision appeared on the front page of tomorrow's newspaper?" This is not a reputation-management tool — it is an integrity diagnostic that forces you to see your decision from the perspective of the constituents you serve. An answer that passes the Newspaper Test under public scrutiny is usually the right answer. An answer that only works in private is usually not.

Yerkes-Dodson — Managing Arousal for Peak Performance

◆ YERKES-DODSON CURVE — THE AROUSAL-PERFORMANCE RELATIONSHIP
Why "Calm Yourself Down" Is Usually Wrong Advice
The Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U describes the relationship between your physiological arousal and your performance. Too little arousal and you underperform through lack of engagement. Too much and anxiety collapses your cognitive access to the frameworks you've built. The target is moderate arousal — alert, present, activated but not overwhelmed. This isn't "calm yourself down." It's "get yourself to optimal."
Low Arousal
Underperformance. Not enough activation to access full recall or execute structured responses. Causes: treating the board as low-stakes, insufficient practice arousal (preparing alone vs. observed). The SPARR "Run It Live" step exists specifically to address this zone.
Optimal Zone ✓
Peak performance. Full recall accessible. Framework automaticity engaged. Your presence and identity are visible to the panel. You're alert, energized, and focused — not calm. Get yourself into this zone before the first question is asked. The Reset Protocol brings you back here when disrupted.
High Arousal
Anxiety-induced performance collapse. Framework access degrades. Rambling replaces structure. Candidates begin monitoring themselves ("how am I doing?") instead of executing. The Four-Step Reset Protocol exists for this zone — to recover framework access within seconds.
If you've been preparing only in low-arousal conditions — reading alone, thinking through responses in your head — you will underperform under board arousal, even if you know every framework cold. SPARR's "Run It Live" step — observed, timed, standing — isn't optional. It's the only preparation that actually trains at the arousal level the board produces.

The Four-Step Reset Protocol

At some point during your board, you will lose your place. The mind goes blank, a question catches you off-guard, or anxiety spikes mid-response. It happens to everyone. The Four-Step Reset Protocol is the recovery sequence that returns you to framework execution within seconds — if you've practiced it beforehand.

◆ FOUR-STEP RESET PROTOCOL — IN-ASSESSMENT RECOVERY
When You Lose Your Place — The Recovery Sequence
This protocol is practiced in SPARR reps until it runs automatically. If you've never practiced recovery, you'll improvise — and improvisation under high arousal produces rambling. If you've rehearsed the Reset Protocol, you'll recover in four steps that take approximately three seconds.
1
Breath — One controlled, visible exhale. This is not metaphorical — it is physiological regulation. One breath lowers arousal from the collapse zone back toward optimal. It also signals to the board that you are composing yourself deliberately, which reads as confidence rather than panic.
2
L·E·A·D — Run the four questions internally in silence: What is the question bucket? What is the primary framework? Am I answering at the correct rank? What TCHOEDS value will close this? This takes three to five seconds. The panel interprets this pause as thoughtfulness. It is. Let them.
3
First Sentence — Commit to one opening sentence that names the framework and signals the structure: "I'd approach this using the 3 U's Diagnostic before any action — let me walk through that." Once the first sentence is out, the structure carries the response. The panic is almost always in the gap before the first sentence, not in the content that follows.
4
Trust the Structure — Execute the framework and stop monitoring yourself. "How am I doing?" is the question that destroys performance. The answer is: follow the structure you have practiced. The board is not evaluating your self-assessment in real time — they are evaluating your response. Be in the response.

The Five Recovery Protocols

◆ FIVE RECOVERY PROTOCOLS — IN-ASSESSMENT DISRUPTIONS
Specific Responses to the Most Common Board Room Failures
Each disruption type requires a different recovery. Treating all five with the same "I need a moment to think" response leaves performance on the table. Know the specific protocol for each disruption type — and practice them in SPARR reps until they are automatic.
Protocol 1 — The Blank
Mind Goes Empty
Run the Four-Step Reset. Open with the framework name: "Let me start with the 3 U's Diagnostic — I'll walk through the diagnosis before any action." The framework name is the re-entry point. Once you name the framework, the structure carries you. The blank is almost always pre-framework. Framework access is the cure.
Protocol 2 — The Don't Know
Genuine Knowledge Gap
State it directly and pivot: "I don't have the specific number for that standard in front of me — what I can tell you is the principle that governs my decision here: [name the principle and apply it]." Boards forgive honest uncertainty acknowledged directly. They do not forgive fabrication or evasion. A principled answer without the specific fact scores higher than a specific fact without a principle.
Protocol 3 — The Partial
Incomplete Answer
Acknowledge the limitation explicitly and expand: "I've covered the immediate response and the documentation piece — the element I want to add is the follow-through, which is where accountability actually lives." Naming what you're adding signals structure and self-awareness. It also often scores the TCHOEDS close that incomplete responses miss.
Protocol 4 — The Supplemental Pivot
Follow-Up Changes Direction
When a follow-up question challenges your answer or takes the scenario in a new direction, use the transition: "That's an important dimension I want to address — let me apply the [framework] to this new element." This signals analytical flexibility — the ability to extend structure to a new variable — which is an executive-level communication skill the board is watching for.
Protocol 5 — The Time Request
Need More Thinking Time
It is always acceptable to say: "I want to take just a moment to organize this — I want to give you a complete answer." Then run L·E·A·D internally. Boards grant this without penalty — it reads as preparation discipline, not confusion. Rushing an unstructured answer to avoid the pause will score lower than taking five seconds to open with a framework.
All Five — Common Thread
Framework Re-Entry Is Always the Solution
Every recovery protocol returns you to a named framework. The framework is the re-entry point because it is the structure that carries the response. When you practice recovery protocols in SPARR reps will find them automatic under board arousal. If you read about them but never practice them, you’ll improvise — and improvisation under high arousal rarely produces 4/5 or 5/5 responses.
