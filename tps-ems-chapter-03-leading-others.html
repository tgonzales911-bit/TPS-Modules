Communications —
The Officer's Primary Tool

⏱ 15 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 113 Buckets

If there is one competency that separates good EMS leaders from great ones — one that assessors name more frequently than any other when they debrief candidates after assessment centers — it is communication. Not clinical communication, which most experienced paramedics have mastered. Leadership communication: the ability to deliver a message clearly, read an audience accurately, navigate high-stakes conversations without creating damage, and write with the precision that administrative documentation demands.

NEMSMA Pillar 3 opens with Communications because it is the delivery vehicle for every other leadership competency. You can have perfect judgment, deep clinical knowledge, and strong values — and still fail an assessment center because you cannot communicate those things clearly under pressure.

The Five Communication Competencies

E: Ensures the organization sets a standard for clear, concise professional oral communication throughout.
Competency 2
Written Communication
S: Writes clearly and concisely to complete assigned duties professionally.
M: Develops processes to ensure staff writes clearly and concisely, completing all duties professionally.
E: Sets organizational standards for written communication that prevent documentation failure.
Competency 3
Listening Skills
S: Demonstrates good listening skills during job performance.
M: Demonstrates the importance of strong listening skills and educates staff about their benefits.
E: Ensures the organization sets a standard for excellent listening skills throughout the agency.
Competency 4
Presentation Skills
S: Demonstrates professional presentation skills when required.
M: Develops processes to ensure staff uses professional presentation skills when required.
E: Ensures the organization sets standards for professional presentations to internal and external audiences.
Competency 5
Adaptive Communication
S: Adapts communication style to the individual or group being addressed to get the best results.
M: Develops processes to ensure staff adapts communication style effectively across situations and audiences.
E: Ensures the organization develops a standard for adaptive communication that strengthens all external and internal relationships.
Adaptive Communication is the most-scored competency in oral board role plays and conflict scenarios. Assessors are watching whether you change your approach based on who you are talking to — a struggling paramedic, an upset family member, a medical director, a city council member. One register for all audiences is a leadership limitation.

The CALM Framework — EMS Conflict Communication

The CALM framework is the TPS communication structure for high-stakes conversations — personnel conflicts, crew disputes, difficult feedback sessions, anything where the emotional temperature is elevated and the standard leadership communication approach risks escalation rather than resolution.

◆ CALM FRAMEWORK — TPS COMMUNICATION TOOL
Clarify · Ask · Listen · Manage
Four steps for high-stakes conversations. Applies to Bucket 1 — Personnel Performance, Bucket 2 — Interpersonal Conflict, and Bucket 3 — Subordinate Counseling — and any scenario where emotion is elevated before facts are clear.
C
Clarify — Set the Purpose
Begin by clearly establishing why this conversation is happening and what it is designed to accomplish. Not an accusation — a purpose statement. "I need to talk with you because I have a concern about what happened during yesterday's shift, and I want to understand it fully before we discuss next steps." Clarify removes ambiguity and signals that this is a purposeful leadership conversation, not a reactive confrontation.
EMS context: Before any disciplinary or feedback conversation — clarify the purpose and separate the conversation from the clinical incident. Don't run this in the bay right after a difficult call.
A
Ask — Gather Before Concluding
Ask specific, open-ended questions before stating any conclusions or positions. "Walk me through what happened from your perspective." "What was going through your mind at that point?" "Is there anything I should know about that situation that I might not be aware of?" This is Suspending Judgment in action — the officer who Asks before concluding demonstrates the Self-Insight competency in real time.
The most common CALM failure: skipping Ask entirely and going straight to conclusions. Assessors notice immediately. "You told me what you were going to do, but you never asked what actually happened."
L
Listen — Actively and Without Defensiveness
Listening in CALM is not passive. It is active confirmation that you have heard — restatement, acknowledgment, and clarification of what was said before moving to your response. "So what I'm hearing is that you felt the protocol was unclear in that situation and made the best decision you could with the information available. Is that right?" Active listening signals that the officer can hold difficult information without immediately filtering it through defensive conclusions.
EMS context: Providers who feel genuinely heard in a difficult feedback conversation are dramatically more likely to accept the feedback and act on it. Providers who feel processed skip the listening entirely.
M
Manage — Lead to Resolution
After Clarifying purpose, Asking for perspective, and Listening actively — Manage the outcome. This is where the officer's leadership judgment enters: what action is appropriate, what expectations are being set, what support is being offered, what accountability looks like. "Based on what we've discussed, here is what I need from you going forward, here is the support I will provide, and here is how we will follow up." Manage closes the loop with clarity, not ambiguity.
The Manage step must include both accountability AND support. All accountability without support is punitive. All support without accountability is permissive. The Confidence column holds both.
In oral board responses: name the framework. "My approach to this conversation would follow the CALM model — I would begin by Clarifying the purpose of the meeting so the employee knows this is a support conversation as much as a corrective one..." Naming CALM signals structured thinking. It moves your response from 3/5 to 4/5.

Interpersonal Awareness —
Reading the Room

⏱ 13 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 2Buckets 2, 5, 12

Interpersonal Awareness is the perceptual engine underneath every other Leading Others competency. You cannot motivate someone whose emotional state you cannot read. You cannot develop someone whose resistance you cannot understand. You cannot influence someone whose concerns you have not identified. Interpersonal Awareness is not a soft skill — it is the operational intelligence that makes every other leadership tool effective.

In the EMS environment, interpersonal awareness is complicated by the clinical culture. Providers are trained to depersonalize — to manage their emotional responses to difficult calls in real time. The same skill that protects them on scene can mask distress from their supervisors off scene. The EMS officer who reads only the surface is missing the signal.

The Four Interpersonal Awareness Competencies

◆ NEMSMA INTERPERSONAL AWARENESS — PILLAR 3
Four Competencies · The Perceptual Foundation of Leadership
These competencies cannot be faked in assessment centers. Assessors probe them through role plays and scenario follow-up questions specifically designed to reveal whether you actually observe people — or just manage processes.
Competency 1
Awareness of Others
S: Demonstrates an understanding of others and educates staff to understand how actions affect others.
M: Develops processes to ensure staff maintains awareness of how their actions affect others.
E: Ensures the organization develops a culture of interpersonal awareness and responsibility for how actions affect others.
Competency 2
Empathy
S: Demonstrates the importance of empathy in the workplace and educates staff about its benefits.
M: Demonstrates a strong empathetic approach to all work interactions and develops processes to ensure staff does the same.
E: Ensures the organization fosters a culture of empathy in all work interactions.
Competency 3
Service Orientation
S: Demonstrates a strong service-oriented mindset toward staff and customers and educates staff about its importance.
M: Develops processes to ensure staff maintains a strong service-oriented mindset toward their staff and customers.
E: Ensures the organization develops a strong service orientation throughout — defining "customer" broadly to include patients, families, community members, and agency personnel.
Competency 4
Political Acumen
S: Understands the political environment of the organization and uses this understanding to navigate complex interpersonal situations.
M: Demonstrates strong political acumen, educates staff about its importance, and navigates organizational complexity effectively.
E: Demonstrates expert political acumen — understands the external political environment and navigates inter-agency, governmental, and community relationships strategically.
Political Acumen is the competency EMS candidates most underestimate. Every EMS agency operates within a political environment — medical directors, hospital systems, fire departments, county government, private ownership, union contracts. The officer who navigates this landscape skillfully does not avoid it. They understand who holds influence, how decisions actually get made, and how to build coalitions that serve patient care.

The EMS-Specific Interpersonal Environment

EMS operates at the intersection of multiple organizational cultures simultaneously. Fire departments, hospitals, county governments, private operators, volunteer agencies — each has its own norms, its own unwritten rules, and its own informal power structure. The EMS officer who navigates this environment skillfully understands something most candidates miss: in EMS, you are almost always operating in someone else's house.

On scene, you are in the hospital's clinical environment. In budget meetings, you are in municipal government's financial environment. In protocol discussions, you are in the medical director's clinical authority environment. Interpersonal awareness in EMS is partly about reading your own crew — and partly about reading every external environment your organization intersects with.

The Role Play Diagnostic — What Interpersonal Awareness Looks Like Live

In an assessment center role play, interpersonal awareness is visible within the first sixty seconds. Did you notice the assessor's emotional state before launching into your prepared response? Did you adapt when they gave you non-verbal cues that your approach wasn't landing? Did you ask a follow-up question when something felt off? The candidate who demonstrates interpersonal awareness in role plays is the one who treats the assessor-actor as a real person, not a scenario prompt to respond to.

Motivating Others —
HARD Goals + What Drives EMS Providers

⏱ 16 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 3Bucket 7 — Goal Setting & Vision

Most EMS officers know the SMART goal framework. Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-Bound — it is the standard goal-setting architecture taught in virtually every leadership program. SMART goals are necessary. They are not sufficient. An officer who can write a SMART goal but cannot make a provider care about achieving it has the structure without the fuel.

The HARD Goals framework answers the question SMART does not: what makes a goal worth pursuing? SMART describes the architecture of a goal. HARD describes the motivational charge that powers someone to pursue it under difficulty, through fatigue, past the point where it would be easier to stop. EMS leaders who master both frameworks have the complete motivational toolkit.

HARD Goals — The Motivational Framework

◆ HARD GOALS FRAMEWORK — NEW IN EMS TRACK
Heartfelt · Animated · Required · Difficult
The motivational architecture underneath every SMART goal. Use in Bucket 7 — Goal Setting & Vision and Bucket 8 — Mentoring & Development oral board responses.
H
Heartfelt — Connected to What Matters
A Heartfelt goal is one the provider is personally invested in achieving — not just assigned to pursue. It connects to their values, their identity as a clinician, or their care for the patients they serve. An EMS officer who can help a provider see why a goal matters personally — not just professionally — has engaged the deepest motivational driver.
EMS application: "I want you to bring your ROSC rate above the national benchmark because you told me you became a paramedic to save lives. The data says you're already close. Let's close that gap together."
A
Animated — Vivid and Compelling
An Animated goal is one the provider can see clearly. They can visualize what success looks like, what it feels like, what the outcome produces. Vague goals ("improve your patient communication") produce vague effort. Animated goals ("by next quarter, your patient complaints are zero and your satisfaction scores are in the top 20% of the unit") produce concrete behavioral change.
EMS application: Help providers visualize the specific outcome — the family that calls to thank their crew, the cardiac arrest that goes home from the hospital, the shift that runs without a single documentation error.
R
Required — Non-Negotiable Standard
A Required goal communicates that this is not optional — it is the standard the organization, the medical director, and the patients depend on. Required framing removes the mental option of deciding whether to pursue the goal and replaces it with the question of how to pursue it. This is not punitive framing — it is honest framing about what professional performance requires.
EMS application: "Protocol compliance is not a preference — it is the foundation of every patient interaction we have. Here is what getting there looks like and how I will support you."
D
Difficult — Worthy of the Effort
HARD Goals are not easy. They are challenging enough to require genuine growth. NEMSMA research shows that people are more motivated by goals that stretch them than by goals that only confirm existing competence. A Difficult goal signals trust — the officer is telling the provider: "I believe you are capable of more than you are currently doing." That message is itself motivating.
EMS application: "I am setting this standard because I know what you are capable of. Easy goals don't develop you. This one will."
In oral board responses, name both frameworks explicitly. "I would set goals for this provider using both the SMART and HARD frameworks. SMART ensures the goal has structure and measurability. HARD ensures the provider has a genuine reason to pursue it beyond compliance. A goal that is SMART but not HARD produces technical compliance — not development."

SMART + HARD — The Complete Goal-Setting Response

When a Bucket 7 — Goal Setting & Vision or Bucket 8 — Mentoring & Development question arises in an oral board, the 4/5 response uses both frameworks and names them. The 3/5 response describes the behavior of good goal-setting without the framework vocabulary. The 2/5 response gives a generic answer about "clear expectations" and "follow-up."

SMART Framework
The Architecture
Specific: Named behavior, not category. Measurable: Data-anchored. Attainable: Challenging but achievable. Relevant: Connected to mission. Time-Bound: Deadline-driven. Without SMART structure, a HARD goal is an aspiration without a plan.
HARD Framework
The Fuel
Heartfelt: Why does this matter to you? Animated: Can you see what success looks like? Required: This is the professional standard. Difficult: I believe you can do more. Without HARD motivation, a SMART goal produces compliance — not commitment.

Developing Others —
The Coaching Cycle in EMS

⏱ 14 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 4Bucket 8 — Mentoring & Development

Developing Others is the competency that most directly separates the provider identity from the officer identity. Providers develop themselves. Officers develop others. This is the core of what the promotion is asking you to do — and it is the most commonly underperformed competency in EMS oral boards because candidates confuse describing good development with demonstrating the thinking of an officer who actually does it.

The Four Developing Others Competencies

◆ NEMSMA DEVELOPING OTHERS — PILLAR 3
Four Competencies · The Coaching Officer
Development is not training. Training is event-based. Development is ongoing, relationship-based, and outcome-accountable.
Competency 1
Coaching
S: Provides coaching and mentoring to staff as needed, developing them to exceed expectations. Identifies opportunities for staff to grow in experience, knowledge, and skills.
M: Develops processes for coaching and mentoring throughout the organization.
E: Ensures the organization maintains high standards of coaching and mentoring as a core cultural value.
Competency 2
Staff Development
S: Develops a training and development plan for all staff under supervision. Provides training and development to all staff while ensuring standards are maintained.
M: Develops processes to ensure all staff receive individualized training and development plans.
E: Ensures the organization develops and maintains a comprehensive, individualized approach to staff development.
Competency 3
Succession Planning
S: Develops and mentors staff to prepare them for future leadership roles within the organization.
M: Develops a process to prepare staff for supervisory and management positions.
E: Ensures the organization maintains a systematic succession plan to prepare staff for all levels of management and leadership.
Competency 4
Delegating
S: Understands and practices effective delegation — assigns duties to staff to allow them to develop new skills and competencies.
M: Delegates effectively and develops processes to ensure supervisors delegate appropriately.
E: Ensures the organization develops a culture of effective delegation as a primary development tool.
Delegation is the development competency most often described as "giving work away." Officers with limited confidence on this competency say: "I like to handle things myself to make sure they're done right." Assessors hear: "I don't trust my crew, I haven't developed them, and I am a bottleneck." Delegation is the most direct behavioral signal of whether you have made the provider-to-officer identity shift.

The Coaching Conversation — Applied to EMS

Coaching in the EMS context happens in three distinct settings, each requiring a slightly different approach. The officer who understands these distinctions and can describe them explicitly in an oral board is demonstrating S-level competency — skill application, not just knowledge recall.

Post-Call Coaching
Real-Time, Specific, Brief
Immediate feedback connected to a specific call or interaction. Three elements: name the specific behavior, name the impact, name the development expectation. Maximum five minutes. Done privately, never in front of crew or patients.
Scheduled 1:1 Coaching
Developmental, Pattern-Focused
Recurring conversation connected to a development plan. Addresses performance patterns, not single incidents. SMART + HARD goal review. The officer brings data; the employee brings self-assessment. Together they build the next development cycle.
Informal Coaching
Opportunistic, Culture-Building
Station conversations, ride-alongs, case reviews during downtime. The officer who uses these moments intentionally — turning a quiet shift into a mentoring opportunity — is developing crew capability at zero additional cost to the organization.
The Succession Planning Question — Executive-Level Application

Executive Officer candidates frequently receive a question about succession planning — either directly ("What is your approach to developing your organization's next generation of leaders?") or embedded in a scenario. The 4/5 response names three elements: a formal succession planning process with identified high-potential employees, an individualized development pathway for each one, and a leadership pipeline that ensures continuity across all supervisory and management positions. The close: "The best organizational leaders I have seen are the ones who are making themselves replaceable. My job is to develop officers who are ready to do what I do — and then move the organization forward past where I could take it alone."

Influencing —
Leading Without Formal Authority

⏱ 13 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 5Bucket 10 — Change Management · Bucket 13 — Situational / Scene

EMS officers operate in one of the most complex authority environments in public safety. On scene, the highest medical authority is the medical director — not the chief. In inter-agency responses, NIMS protocols govern who is actually in command. In budget negotiations, elected officials or private owners hold the financial authority. At the hospital, clinical staff and administration hold their own hierarchy. An EMS officer who can only lead within their formal chain of command will be ineffective in most of the situations that actually matter.

Influencing is the competency that closes that gap. It is the ability to move people toward outcomes you care about without relying on positional authority to make them comply.

The Four Influencing Competencies

◆ NEMSMA INFLUENCING — PILLAR 3
Four Competencies · Credibility-Based Leadership
The officer with high Influencing skill does not need to invoke authority. Their credibility, communication, and coalition-building do the work.
Competency 1
Persuasion
S: Demonstrates effective persuasion techniques and educates staff on their importance.
M: Demonstrates advanced persuasion skills and develops processes to ensure staff uses effective persuasion in their leadership roles.
E: Ensures the organization values and develops persuasion skills to build strategic partnerships and advance organizational goals.
Competency 2
Negotiation
S: Demonstrates effective negotiation skills and educates staff on their importance.
M: Demonstrates effective negotiation and develops processes to ensure staff uses negotiation appropriately when needed.
E: Ensures the organization values and develops negotiation skills — particularly in contract negotiations, mutual aid agreements, and inter-agency partnerships.
Competency 3
Coalition Building
S: Understands the importance of coalition building and educates staff about its benefits.
M: Develops processes to build coalitions within and outside the organization.
E: Ensures the organization maintains active coalitions with key stakeholders — hospitals, fire departments, county government, community health partners — to advance patient care goals.
Competency 4
Networking
S: Demonstrates the importance of networking and educates staff about its benefits for professional development.
M: Builds and maintains a professional network and develops processes to ensure staff does the same.
E: Ensures the organization builds and maintains a comprehensive network of professional relationships that advances the mission and positions the agency as a leader in the EMS field.
Coalition Building is the most EMS-specific influencing competency. EMS agencies are rarely standalone — they operate within a system of hospitals, fire departments, county EMS offices, state EMS bureaus, and community health organizations. The Executive EMS Officer who has built genuine coalitions across this landscape can accomplish in a phone call what others could not accomplish through formal authority in months.

Influence in Practice — EMS-Specific Scenarios

Protocol Change
Medical Director Coalition
EMS officers cannot unilaterally change clinical protocols. Protocol change requires medical director buy-in, often hospital system alignment, and sometimes state EMS bureau approval. The officer who presents data-driven proposals, builds clinical credibility over time, and demonstrates the outcomes case is practicing Influencing at its highest level.
Budget Defense
Elected Official Persuasion
When budget requests go before city council, county commissioners, or a board of directors, the EMS officer is leading without any operational authority over the decision-makers. Persuasion here means translating clinical necessity into community impact language — connecting ambulance replacement to response time data to patient outcomes to constituent satisfaction.
Inter-Agency Operations
Unified Command Influence
In unified command with a fire department IC, the EMS branch director has no authority over fire resources. Influence means presenting the medical dimension of the scene so clearly and compellingly that fire resources are allocated in alignment with patient care priorities — not because the EMS officer ordered it.
Resistant Senior Crew
Peer Credibility + Persuasion
The newly promoted EMS lieutenant leading a crew of senior paramedics has nominal authority and zero earned credibility. Influencing through demonstrated clinical knowledge, consistent fairness, and genuine investment in crew success is the only path that actually works. Invoking formal authority too early here damages the relationship permanently.

Human Resources —
Legal Foundations + Personnel Leadership

⏱ 15 min📘 Pillar 3 · Sub-section 6Bucket 1 — Personnel Performance · Bucket 3 — Subordinate Counseling · Bucket 4 — Policy & Ethics

Human Resources Management is the competency cluster that most directly exposes the gap between officers who know leadership principles and officers who can actually implement them within the legal and organizational constraints that govern the employment relationship. In EMS, this gap has direct patient safety implications: an officer who cannot navigate progressive discipline effectively, manage leave policies legally, or conduct an investigation without contaminating it is an organizational liability regardless of their clinical excellence.

NEMSMA is explicit that HR knowledge at the Managing and Executive levels includes substantial legal literacy — employment law, labor relations, EEO compliance, and the procedural requirements that turn leadership intentions into defensible organizational actions.

The Five HR Management Competencies

◆ NEMSMA HR MANAGEMENT — PILLAR 3
Five Competencies · From Supervision to Employment Law
HR Management scales from personnel supervision at Supervising to organizational policy and legal compliance at Executive.
Competency 1
Personnel Management
S: Manages direct reports professionally and fairly — scheduling, performance tracking, corrective action. M: Develops processes to ensure all supervisors manage personnel professionally and consistently. E: Ensures the organization maintains comprehensive personnel management standards that protect employees and the organization.
Competency 2
Labor Relations
S: Understands and complies with labor contracts and union agreements where applicable. M: Demonstrates understanding of labor relations and ensures supervisory compliance. E: Manages the organization's relationship with labor representation at the strategic level — contract negotiations, grievance processes, partnership development.
Competency 3
Employment Law
S: Understands basic employment law as it applies to supervisory decisions. M: Understands employment law broadly and ensures supervisors receive training to maintain compliance. E: Ensures the organization maintains comprehensive employment law compliance and proactively addresses legal risk before incidents occur.
Competency 4
EEO Compliance
S: Understands and complies with EEO requirements in all supervisory decisions. M: Ensures EEO compliance throughout the division and trains supervisors on requirements. E: Builds organizational culture that values diversity and inclusion as both legal requirements and operational assets — ensuring EEO compliance is embedded in hiring, promotion, and performance management systems.
Competency 5
Discipline and Accountability
S: Applies progressive discipline consistently and documentably — verbal counseling, written warning, suspension, termination — according to agency policy and Just Culture principles. M: Develops processes to ensure all supervisors apply discipline consistently, legally, and documentably. E: Ensures the organization maintains a progressive discipline system that is fair, legally defensible, and aligned with Just Culture principles that distinguish between system failures and individual negligence.
The most common HR failure in EMS oral boards is not illegal behavior — it is inconsistency. Officers who apply discipline differently to different employees based on personal relationships, seniority, or likability are creating equal employment liability even when no individual action is discriminatory. Consistent, documented, policy-aligned discipline is the standard. Name it that way in your responses.

The Four Types of Justice — Fairness in HR Decisions

When HR scenarios arise in oral boards, the officer who can name the type of fairness at stake — and speak to it explicitly — is demonstrating S-level performance. The Four Types of Justice provide the analytical framework for any fairness or discipline question.

◆ FOUR TYPES OF JUSTICE — EMS HR FRAMEWORK
The Fairness Architecture in Personnel Decisions
Name the type of justice at stake before describing your response. This is the A (Apply) step in L·E·A·D for any Bucket 1 — Personnel Performance, Bucket 3 — Subordinate Counseling, or Bucket 4 — Policy & Ethics question.
DJ
Distributive Justice — Are Outcomes Fair?
The degree to which outcomes are perceived as fair relative to what was invested or relative to what others in similar situations received. When employees believe discipline was disproportionate — "why did they get suspended and I only got a warning for the same thing?" — they are raising a Distributive Justice concern.
In oral boards: "My response addresses the Distributive Justice dimension — I need to ensure that the consequence is proportional to the behavior and consistent with how similar situations have been handled."
IJ
Interpersonal Justice — Were They Treated With Respect?
The degree to which people feel they were treated with dignity and respect during a process. An employee can accept a negative outcome if they felt heard, respected, and treated with professionalism throughout. An employee who receives a technically correct discipline with dismissiveness or disrespect experiences Interpersonal Justice failure regardless of the outcome's accuracy.
In oral boards: "The Interpersonal Justice dimension requires that regardless of the outcome of this process, this employee is treated with dignity and professionalism throughout. That is not negotiable."
InfoJ
Informational Justice — Were They Told Why?
The degree to which people receive adequate explanations for decisions that affect them. Employees who receive corrective action or policy changes without understanding the reasoning behind them experience Informational Justice failure. Transparency about why a decision was made — even when the decision is negative — dramatically increases perceived fairness.
In oral boards: "The Informational Justice element means I need to clearly explain what the performance standard is, how this behavior fell below it, and what the reasoning behind the corrective action is."
PJ
Procedural Justice — Was the Process Fair?
The degree to which the process used to reach a decision was fair, consistent, and unbiased. Employees may accept outcomes they disagree with if they believe the process was fair. This is why consistent application of progressive discipline policy is so important — the process is the protection, for the employee and the organization simultaneously.
In oral boards: "The Procedural Justice element requires that I follow agency policy consistently — same process, same documentation, same standards — regardless of the individual involved or the personal relationships at stake."
